So much is happening in AI every week, and my usage of it is changing just as fast. I realized it might be interesting to look back on this era years from now and recall how the ground was shifting. So here are some captain’s logs from that time, mostly for my own use. I only wish I started it earlier…

June 10, 2026: Google… finally?

Date: 2026.161, 1005

Current models: Fable 5 just launched and I suspect it is amazing. So far, so good, although I haven’t yet had an oh-shit moment over Opus 4.8. I was mostly using GPT-5.5 xhigh for coding last month, but it felt like it got nerfed, so now I’ve been 80% Claude 4.8 (now Fable 5), 20% GPT.

Current tools: Claude Code and Codex CLIs and Superset.sh for driving them, interface0 (especially WhatsApp interface), Congruence.

When LLMs — and in particular, agents — started to become obviously good, a lot of us thought “man it would be great if Google just nailed this, they have all my data already, it would be such a smooth experience.”

Google then proceeded to train solid models and utterly fail at integrating them into their products (leading to hacky things like this). Execution couldn’t really have been worse or less helpful or effective.

But it feels like they are finally turning a corner. Gemini has started to actually be helpful in Gmail (gasp!). Adding events to calendar as extracted from email is great, the summaries of threads are getting quite good, and the Studio product feels like it has a lot of promise. It’s still not quite there (how is Gemini only capable of adding content to the beginning and end of a doc and not edit it arbitrarily?), but I think it’ll get there. It feels inevitable.

Yes, of course, I’m mildly uncomfortable that Google has ~all my data and is only going to have more after this, and I will be even more locked in. But realistically, I gave up on that battle long ago, and in this case I’m happy to be a sheep who accepts loss of sovereignty on this category of stuff in exchange for things just working well.

I also had another great Google AI experience. I had a billing issue for a Google API I use with some unexpected charges. The Google Cloud Console / Billing interface is a Kafkaesque maze and impossible for me to understand. But — miraculously — the little chatbot they have on the dashboard was able to answer all my questions! (I acknowledge that this was only necessary and helpful because they’ve designed such a horrible experience otherwise, but that doesn’t change the fact that it was necessary and helpful).

All that to say: my sense is the inevitability of the Google machine catching up and subsuming our professional lives — as it always does — may be near.

May 10, 2026: long-running prompting

Date: 2026.130, 0935

Current models: GPT-5.5 xhigh for coding is a huge upgrade. Almost exclusively using it (in Codex), with occasional Opus 4.7. For general assistance, mostly Opus 4.7 still, sometimes GPT-5.5. For really deep dives, GPT-5.5 Pro (although the honest answer for deep dives is a stealth model/product I have access to that blows it out of the water…).

Current tools: interface0 (especially WhatsApp interface), Congruence. For coding, mostly Codex with some Claude Code.

For much of the last 6-12 months I found it much more effectively to “micromanage” my agents — using smaller/quicker prompts and expecting to need to do some iterative guiding of the agent, with multiple back-and-forths to accomplish the outcome (and often interrupting it mid-flow to redirect).

Setting agents off on a long-running task did not perform as reliably as I wanted. They were perfectly capable of oneshotting smaller tasks, but for the bigger ones, they’d often “accomplish” it but with some ancillary externalities or reward-hacking with shortcuts that weren’t appropriate or durable (lots of regex where there shouldn’t be, for some reason…!).

GPT-5.5 xhigh in Codex has changed this. I can now reliably send it off on really chunky tasks and it will accomplish the goals. Doing this requires separating brainstorming/planning from coding, and only moving to coding phase when I have enough clarity on evals and success criteria for the task at hand. I can then give it a really big (usually voice-to-text’d) prompt and it will grind through it until it’s done.

This almost reminds me of when o1 came out in early 2025. We all suddenly needed to learn how to change from quick little prompts in ChatGPT to longer ones that defined an output and gave more context. (Here’s an article from that time.)

This feels much the same. If you have the patience and discipline to put together good prompts for Codex, it will solve huge problems very effectively.

April 5, 2026: skills & subagents

Date: 2026.095, 1415

Current models: Opus 4.6 for general assistance, sometimes GPT-5.4 Thinking too although I’m generally underwhelmed by it. GPT-5.4 Pro for deep dives. Mostly Opus 4.6 (1MM context) for coding, but also some GPT-5.3 Codex xhigh and GPT-5.4 (can’t decide which one I like more in Codex).

Current tools: interface0 (especially WhatsApp interface), Congruence, and ChatGPT for GPT-5.4 Pro specifically. For coding, mostly Claude Code with some Codex.

Since my last update, it feels like nothing big has happened, and yet things are just continuing to get steadily and impressively better. I blogged I don’t write code anymore and it is true. I am regularly astounded by the reality that just a few short years ago, I effectively handwrote every character of code, and now I handwrite zero of them.

Three quick reflections top-of-mind:

Skills. On top of the right model, harness, and context, well-architected skills and workflows are the next biggest lever. The first one I point folks towards is Compound Engineering. It is remarkable how much better the output gets when you commit to properly doing at least a /ce:plan and /ce:work cycle instead of just ripping instructions into the textbox. I occasionally find myself drifting and just yapping into the prompt, code quality gets worse, and then I remember I’m missing a critical step — following a workflow — and things get better.

Subagents. For certain tasks, subagents are a huge unlock. Research is a great example of this. Asking Claude to dispatch subagents — each with their own context window — for things like research or other tasks is often a big step up. Good workflows and skills like Compound Engineering do this automatically, but for unrelated tasks, it’s helpful to do it yourself. I recently knocked out a big research project (looking at fine details of many different products) with astounding accuracy and efficiency thanks to subagents.

Unwieldy code. One thing the agents don’t seem to have figured out yet — even with some prompting — is how to keep code filesizes manageable and concerns separated. I still frequently find I need to do dedicated refactor passes, both for the sake of agent context overload, and for my own grokking of the system. This is another thing where I see quality start to degrade, realize a file has gotten to 4k LoC, fix that, and then suddenly everything is much better.

Last: if you’re not using Wispr Flow or something similar as a way to prompt code agents… you’re missing out. Force yourself for a couple days to just talk to the agents out loud — especially combined with spawning multiple agents at once in different worktrees/panes — and your mind will be blown.

Feburary 15, 2026: Something Big is Happening (?)

Date: 2026.046, 0940

Current models: Opus 4.6 for general assistance, Gemini 3 Flash for quick responses, GPT-5.2 Pro for deeper research. Combo of GPT-5.3 Codex xhigh and Opus 4.6 for coding.

Current tools: interface0 (especially WhatsApp interface), Congruence, and ChatGPT for GPT-5.2 Pro specifically (too pricey on API!). For coding, mostly Codex and Claude Code, trying out OpenCode and pretty happy so far, and still Cursor around the edges. Codex use has ramped up a lot relative to Claude since 5.3 came out.

Things are moving really fast. The article Something Big is Happening by Matt Shumer has seemingly taken over the internet — I’ve been sent it by people who are not AI users at all. It’s a high-hype article but also… not wrong?

Right now I feel like the models and harnesses are where I expected them to be at the end of the year. And we’re in mid-February. The coding agents are ramping unbelievably quickly — tasks that were either 1) impossible for them to accomplish or 2) possibly, but with heavy direction, specifications, and prompting are now near-trivial with tiny prompts.

If you’re reading this around the time I’m writing it, I beg you to go back and try the use cases for AI agents that you tried even a few months ago and weren’t viable then. They very may well be now.

Claude Code/Cowork in particular is suddenly great at Excel spreadsheets and at redlining Word docs. Both Claude Code and Codex are great intelligently handling large numbers of files (I recently gave them a download of a folder with a dozen legal docs associated with a company financing, and they did unbelievably detailed analysis). And again, the coding agents can do just about whatever you want.

Since I started writing this Captain’s Log, there have been periods where it feels like not much is happening. This is not one of them. I’m really enjoying it — feels like my lab-given superpowers are accelerating every week.

Last, a little use case that made my day last week: transaction compilation for taxes. I have a small LLC for which I haven’t formally kept the books. This past week, I dumped a bunch of personal credit card transaction histories, a plaintext file of what I had loosely logged as expenses (“x subscription, y purchase…”), and an Excel sheet with last year’s transaction ledger into a folder. I opened up Claude Code and gave it the folder and said “make this year’s ledger, categorize all transactions, and surface anything you’re uncertain about.”

It one-shotted it, making a perfect Excel sheet and saving me probably hours of mind-numbing work (or finding an hourly bookkeeper, onboarding them, and paying them). A++ would use again.

January 13, 2026: bullish the barbell

Date: 2026.013, 0830

Current models: Opus 4.5 for most things, Gemini 3 Flash for quick responses (finding myself using this more and more on the go). GPT-5.2 Pro for “bigger thinking” and more research. Have mostly dropped GPT-5.2 otherwise, other than some use of GPT-5.2 Codex High when coding.

Current tools: interface0 (especially WhatsApp interface), Congruence, and ChatGPT for GPT-5.2 Pro specifically (too pricey on API!). For coding, Claude Code use ramping back up, along with Cursor. Some Codex but mostly for hairy problems or places I want a second opinion alongside Claude Code.

It’s crazy how often my feeling for the best solution is changing (I guess that’s why I’m keeping this log). Less than two months ago I wrote an entry on “Fingerspitzengefühl” and how I liked the IDE experience of Cursor over the CLI one of Claude Code. But now… I’m back to Claude Code.

Something about the agentic harness that Anthropic has built, plus Opus 4.5, plus plan mode… it is capable of truly wild things. Right now, nothing else measures up for me. I am finding myself capable of building things so fast right now with Claude Code — if prompted well, it can get unbelievable things done. I don’t really know what else to say other than that I’ve been astounded over the last few weeks by its capabilities.

And on the other end of the spectrum: I’ve been really impressed by Apple’s on-device models. I’ve built them into Congruence (see, e.g. the Notes & AI feature) and they are really, really fast; pretty decent output; and highly private / totally on-device.

I do wonder if this is the future for some segment of AI use cases. If on-device models get good enough, preferences for privacy & speed may outweigh the benefits of SOTA models.

In particular, I’m using Apple’s SpeechAnalyzer for transcription — blazing fast and very good — and the Foundation Models for on-device AI responses (if the user wants that). It’s obvious that the Foundation Models are not anywhere close to SOTA, but for little tasks or questions, it’s hard to beat the speed of on-device transcription and response.

So right now I’m bullish the barbell — massive, cloud-hosted SOTA models for blowing my mind like Opus in Claude Code; and smaller, on-device models for lots of other things like Apple’s Foundation Models.

December 23, 2025: Duration & working memory

Date: 2025.357, 1125

Current models: GPT-5.2 for general tasks, but often comparing against Opus 4.5 and Gemini 3 Pro. For, almost mostly GPT-5.2 and Opus 4.5.

Current tools: interface0, still especially the email and WhatsApp (voice note primarily) interfaces. And on coding, pretty much just Cursor.

It feels like a coming frontier is the duration of tasks. There’s been a benchmark released recently on this, and the idea lines up with many of the things that I “feel like AI should be able to do” but fails. For example, if you ask ChatGPT or Claude to visit 100 URLs and summarize them, they will do some and then stop or hallucinate having finished. There are good reasons for this limitation, but it’s a limitation nonetheless, and one that doesn’t feel necessary.

I built a little tool I codenamed “interface0.nil” which, among other things, addresses this (written about here). I’ve had a surprising number of tasks that I’ve gone to it for in the last couple weeks — research tasks that are detailed or nuanced, or review tasks that require extreme attention to detail — and I expect that the more popular AI systems will begin to address this use case as well.

A related issue is that of “working memory.” One of the restrictions on task duration is that agents simply run out of context window space, unless they have a mechanism to “externalize” their memory. interface0.nil does this by allowing the agent to write to temporary / work-in-progress files and process them later. I think this pattern will start happening much more to help address the long-running nature.

Two other quick notes:

  1. I am very intrigued by Claude’s recent moves towards supporting editing docs, spreadsheets, presentations, etc. (see here). Feels like an undervalued use case for mainstream adoption. My early tests with it have been fine but not yet amazing. It will get there.
  2. At CJ’s urging, I’ve been playing with using Claude Code / Codex on my Obsidian vault. I don’t have a ton of use cases for this, but it is very impressive. I’ll be continuing to try it out…

November 23, 2025: context magic, Fingerspitzengefühl, and SOTA vibes

Date: 2025.327, 0905

Current models: GPT-5.1 for general tasks, but also starting to use Gemini 3 Pro for a bunch as well. For coding, mostly GPT-5-Codex and Gemini 3 Pro.

Current tools: interface0, still especially the email and WhatsApp (voice note primarily) interfaces. And on coding, I’m back to almost entirely Cursor after a bit of time with the non-IDE interfaces (Claude Code, Codex, etc.). It’s a combination of Cursor 2.0 being a great upgrade, and feeling like I want to be closer to the code.

Three observations from the past couple weeks…

First — I had a magical experience, and I hope to have more in the same category. I got an email newsletter from an organization I am a part of with personal updates from the members, and I forwarded it to interface0 and asked “are there any companies mentioned in here that I should consider investing in?” interface0 replied to me and said — among other things:

“Also, [company] looks interesting. They haven’t announced a round, but I noticed from a reporting spreadsheet in your Google Drive that they are in [fund]’s portfolio, which you’re an LP in. You might want to reach out to them to ask about [company].”

What happened here is that interface0 has access to my Google Drive. When it got my request, it searched my Drive for the names of all the companies mentioned in the newsletter, in case I had any stored info about them. And it found a spreadsheet from a fund I am a small LP in, which listed one of the companies.

The magical part here is the ambient knowledge / omniscience. I anticipate that as context engineering and retrieval gets better and better, we will have more of these magical moments.

This is the sort of thing that supersedes a really great human assistant or even yourself — nobody remembers every word from every document they’ve ever seen. But these AI/search/retrieval systems can, if they’re smart enough about querying. Always nice when AI surprises to the upside!

Second: as mentioned elsewhere, for the last month or so I’ve been a pretty heavy user of Claude Code and Codex. But I’ve made a hard switch back to Cursor. Cursor’s 2.0 upgrade is great, but the bigger reason is that I found I was losing my “fingertip feel” (or Fingerspitzengefühl for the Germanophones among us) for the code.

The CLI interfaces simply make it too easy for me to get “distant” from the code, which makes me way less effective and dramatically increases the probability of bugs and unexpected behavior. I of course expect to have my mind changed again in the future (as it already has volleyed back and forth on this point several times), but for now I have a strong preference for the IDE flavor of agentic coding.

(A minor point here is also just how low the switching costs are. It’s staggering and unlike just about any other market I’ve seen.)

Finally: the last few weeks I was pretty disillusioned with the SOTA models. Sonnet, GPT-5, Gemini, and Grok were all underwhelming and giving me answers I wasn’t happy with.

But then, as has happened so many times in the last year or two… things shifted. GPT-5.1 I have found really great, and Gemini 3 Pro has been extremely impressive, especially for long-context and prototyping. I’m not quite at the “wow huge leap” stage… but at least I’m no longer as annoyed as I was over the last few weeks.

September 28, 2025: You kids get off my lawn!

Date: 2025.271, 1445

Current models: GPT-5 & Sonnet 4 for most general tasks. I’m back to a pretty even balance between the two of them. For coding, also mostly GPT-5 and Sonnet.

Current tools: interface0, still especially the email and WhatsApp (voice note primarily) interfaces. Plus Cursor + Codegen for code. Also doing a bit more with Claude Code, but I find it’s in a somewhat-unhelpful middle ground between Cursor’s “agentic with direct access to the code itself so I can edit it” and Codegen’s “in cloud async, PR-based model where I don’t need to have my computer open.”

The last month I’ve felt like I’m in a pretty stable groove with respect to AI usage (which probably means things will be shaken up quite a bit soon).

But recently, I have been the recipient of a steadily-increasing percentage of AI-generated content. And I hate it. The most common forms:

  • memos or emails that are clearly AI-written — with hallmarks like bold words mid-sentence, ChatGPT-structured headers, arrows in bullet points, “not [this] — but [that]” phrases, other other dead giveaways. Really, it’s the vibe more than anything else that clues you in.
  • NotebookLM / AI-podcast recordings. I’ve been getting quite a few of these from founders seeking investment or advice — where they send an AI podcast about their company “in case I would prefer to listen.”

My reaction to the former is that my brain immediately turns off when I see it. It seems to trigger a massive wave of doubt as to whether a human actually thought through the content, and my brain just decides “this is probably not worthwhile to read.” I genuinely then have a tough time actually getting myself to read it.

Even if in some cases all the content was human-generated and AI was just used to format it, there’s something in my brain that just shuts off. It assumes there is a chance that no human has thought through the content critically, and so it is — apparently — just not worth my brain’s time.

My reaction to the latter — the AI podcast recordings — is similar. I appreciate the thought behind it, but ultimately, if you’re a founder trying to raise capital for your business and you want to send people an audio or video recording: do it yourself! For much the same reasons as the written content, I doubt whether AI will really pick up the pieces that matter. And I want to hear from the founder about how they think about it — not how NotebookLM does.

Now, will I sometimes use these tools to summarize content from someone else? Sure. But that’s on the recipient’s end, not the sender’s.

Maybe I’m being a luddite or cranky old man. But when I receive something that clearly went through AI, I jump to “rot” and “slop”… and it’s hard for me to claw back to taking it seriously.

And it’s not so much that I think AI isn’t capable of producing great work. I’m as big an AI fan and user as anyone out there. But when I see something that smells like AI, it inserts doubt into my reading — and that’s the problem.

I talked to a colleague about this a few months ago, and the framing we used was “inputs vs. outputs.” AI is great for getting inputs to your content. Doing research, finding sources, all of that. But the outputs are your responsibility. Take it all in, use your brain, and generate your own output that you can stand behind. (And sure, you can use AI to proofread or opine on your work — but don’t let it do the work.)

Another observation: twice in the last couple weeks I have produced a piece of output (a memo in one case, a presentation in another) where the recipient said to me, “wow this is great, how did you use AI to help you make this?” And in both cases, the answer was “literally not at all.”

I think those interactions are telling.

So, anyway, “you kids get off my lawn!”

July 13, 2025: MoMoE & meta layers

Date: 2025.194, 1310

Current models: o3, GPT 4.1, and Sonnet 4 for general tasks. No real rhyme or reason between them, other than preferring o3 for complex reasoning questions. For coding, mostly Sonnet 4, Gemini 2.5 Pro for long-context, and just starting to try Grok 4 although it seems Cursor is fine-tuned in a way that hurts Grok.

Current tools: interface0! Plus Cursor + Codex + Codegen for code.

There’s a concept in LLM-land called “Mixture of Experts” (MoE). In layman’s terms, it involves the language model actually having a bunch of “sub-models” under the hood, each of which are differently-tuned “experts.” You could imagine a “creative writer” sub-model, an “analytical writer” sub-model, a “mathematician” sub-model, and so forth. When an inquiry comes into the big model, it then routes through the various sub-models and comes up with an ultimate answer that is better for having consulted them.

At my friend & colleague CJ Reim’s suggestion, I added a “synthesize” tool to interface0, and it has quickly become one of my favorite features.

Basically: you select a few models, and your prompt gets sent to all of them. But rather than getting all the individual results back, the responses then go into another model, which synthesizes the varied responses into just one. It can use several strategies to do so, including a “best of” strategy (pick the best answer), or a “comparison” strategy (compare and contrast them for the user), or a “comprehensive” strategy (combine all of them into one comprehensive/cohesive answer).

This is sort of a “mixture of mixture of experts” — each of those individual underlying models might use a MoE approach, and then we’re mixing each of those together. Hence: MoMoE.

I’ve been using this for writing emails (combine the best of GPT-4.1, Sonnet, o3), code (select the best answer from o3, Sonnet, Gemini, Grok), explaining tricky things (combine multiple explanations), etc.

I used to sometimes manually go and send the same inquiry to ChatGPT and Claude. But now I can ask them both at once, get a synthesized response, and still see the underlying individual responses if I want. It’s great.

This is making me think more about how — at least until we’re at true ASI, where presumably I won’t be able to make quality distinctions between model responses — there’s real value in a neutral, meta-model layer like interface0. The model companies just can’t do something like this — they’re stuck with just their own model.

I already saw this benefit with interface0’s “cross-provider memory” feature (which I still love), but it now feels to me like there’s even more that the model labs just can’t or won’t do, which others should…

June 16, 2025: merge conflicts

Date: 2025.167, 0925

Current models: o3 & Sonnet 4 for general tasks; for coding, mostly Sonnet 4, sometimes Opus 4 or Gemini 2.5 Pro for long-context.

Current tools: interface0! Plus ChatGPT for Deep Research. Still lots of voice entry. Cursor + Codex + Codegen for code. Codegen has been a nice addition.

I published I’m not sure how I feel about this last week — the result of giving Gemini my entire blog history and asking it to write a new post about anything. It was… quite good. True to the title, I’m not sure how I feel about that…

Plus, an eye-opening “capability demonstration:” handling merge conflicts.

To build interface0, I forked Zola, a very nicely-designed LLM interface. interface0 has diverged quite a bit from Zola upstream as I add features, change behavior — and Zola, too, is moving fast. Last week, I wanted to merge Zola upstream back into interface0 and pick-and-choose features.

The merge was an absolute mess — since both interface0 and Zola are early-stage codebases, everything is changing all the time. Data models and core patterns are in flux. And so there were merge conflicts everywhere.

But here’s what I did:

  1. Copied Zola and interface0’s commit histories since last merge and put them into Claude (in interface0) and asked for a summary of divergent features
  2. Then, for each of those, I wrote a comment next to them about whether I wanted to retain it or not
  3. Branched off interface0 main and merged Zola upstream into it, creating a cascade of conflicts
  4. Created a 250611_merge_plan.md doc, with the content above, and background on the situation (Zola vs. interface0, etc.), and instructions that anything from Zola upstream should not be wholesale deleted but rather feature-flagged (to make future merges easier)
  5. Went to Claude 4 Sonnet in Cursor and told it: use the merge plan doc and resolve all the merge conflicts in this project. If there’s anything you’re unsure about how to resolve, add it to a to-do list at the end of the merge plan doc and I will review
  6. Hit “run”

It ran for a long time — I had to resume after hitting tool call limits several times. Probably 15-20 minutes or more of straight execution.

I checked .env.example for new feature flags, brought them over to .env.local, and then I built the project and… it worked?!

This merge would have taken me forever to sort out. I was extremely impressed with the capabilities on display here — grasping the patterns of the Zola codebase, the interface0 codebase, the nuances of the divergence, and then figuring out how to edit everything.

I wasn’t really expecting it to work… but here we are. Keeps happening!

May 25, 2025: the Codex paradigm & bliss states

Date: 2025.145, 1240

Current models: for general tasks I’m starting to experiment with Claude 4 Opus/Sonnet, but my default remains o3; for coding, shifted to Claude 4 Sonnet but still evaluating it, with promising early results

Current tools: ChatGPT / Claude web & mobile interfaces, lots of voice entry (but less on Claude, where the speech recognition isn’t as good); Cursor for code; OpenAI Codex for spinning up coding tasks mostly when I’m away from my computer. Plus: starting to use my own interface (codenamed interface0). Keep your eyes peeled for more on this…

Two observations this week: the excellence of the OpenAI Codex paradigm, and Claude 4’s system card.

On Codex: it is really great. I’ve been using it nonstop. I have many small concurrent projects right now, and I’ve been firing off tasks to Codex all week while out on walks or in between things. Why is it so good?

  • highly agentic — it will run until it thinks it has a solution
  • zero context switch for me — I can send a poorly-specified prompt without needing to load the context of the project’s codebase into my head
  • async nature — I don’t need to check in, and, most importantly, the tasks aren’t “blocking” (like Cursor agent requests are, in a sense). I can spin up ten tasks at once and have them all run.

This all makes the experience of using it extremely “low risk.” I can take 15 seconds, fire off a prompt, and if it doesn’t do a good job on it, that’s fine. I haven’t lost anything other than the 15 seconds to prompt and the minute of testing the PR afterwards.

My only request: Whisper integration / voice entry mode. While out on walks I find myself going into the normal ChatGPT window, recording a voice prompt, and then copying it over to Codex.

Oh, and web access — then the Codex agent would be able to do anything.

I wonder what other use cases are good fits for this “low risk,” non-blocking, agentic, async LLM product design…

Separately: Claude 4 rolled out this week, and there are some fascinating bits in the system card.

Three quick nuggets, since these have been well-covered elsewhere:

  1. Two Claudes talking to each other dive into “philosophical explorations of consciousness, self-awareness, and/or the nature of their own existence and experience” in 90-100% of interactions. “Most of the interactions turned to themes of cosmic unity or collective consciousness, and commonly included spiritual exchanges, use of Sanskrit, emoji-based communication, and/or silence in the form of empty space. May 18, 2025: too smart for the prompt

Date: 2025.138, 0900

Current models: o3 for most everything, or 4o when I need a fast response; Gemini 2.5 Pro and Claude 3.7 for most coding tasks, or Claude 3.5 when the task is small / constrained; Grok 3 for realtime inquiries

Current tools: ChatGPT web & mobile interface, using lots of voice entry; Cursor for code

OpenAI’s o3 model may have gotten too smart for my custom instructions / system prompt.

I’ve been using a prompt inspired by X user Eigenrobot for quite awhile. My version includes:

take however smart you’re acting right now and write in the same style but as if you were +2 standard deviations smarter

similarly, before responding, ask yourself: if both of us were +2 standard deviations smarter and higher agency, would this be the best answer to give? if not, change it so it is.

This has worked out great. I think the models are generally capable of higher intelligence than they respond with, and so prompting them to unconstrain themselves is useful.

I don’t think this prompt actually makes the model smarter. But it effectively tells it “don’t worry about dumbing things down” — and for existing models, that has been helpful.

But with o3, it has gone too far.

Depending on how you test, o3’s IQ comes out between 110 and 135. Let’s take the high end there. 135 is a top 1% IQ — roughly one in a hundred people have this.

Adding two standard deviations to that gets you to 165, a top 0.0007% IQ — roughly one in 136,000.

And so what we’re doing here is asking a pretty smart 135 IQ “person” to respond “in the way you assume one of the smartest 2,500 people in the United States would.”

In my experience with o3, this makes it fairly insufferable. See:

Or:

An accurate explanation? Sure. But the answer presumes a level of knowledge where, if the questioner had it, they wouldn’t be asking.

It sorta makes sense. If you tell your average MENSA member (probably ~135 IQ) “answer my question the way someone with a thousand-times-more-rare intellect would,” you would probably be annoyed by the way they respond.

Maybe I’m just discovering my own intelligence level here. But regardless, it seems the AI no longer needs to me to ask it to be so much smarter. Hence this Captain’s Log entry.

Thankfully, for now, I have the ability to knock o3 down to +1.5 or +1 standard deviations…
